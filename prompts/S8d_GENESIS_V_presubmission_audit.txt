PHASE 1 — COMPLETE MANUSCRIPT AUDIT

Read the entire manuscript and all available supplementary materials before making changes.

Evaluate every component:

1. Title.
2. Abstract.
3. Introduction.
4. Literature review.
5. Research gap.
6. Original contribution.
7. Theoretical foundations.
8. Mathematical framework.
9. Research methodology.
10. Experimental design.
11. Statistical analysis.
12. Results.
13. Discussion.
14. Limitations.
15. Conclusion.
16. References.
17. Tables and figures.
18. Supplementary materials.

Identify every issue that could weaken the manuscript.

Pay particular attention to:

- Logical inconsistencies.
- Unsupported claims.
- Redundant explanations.
- Ambiguous terminology.
- Mathematical errors.
- Statistical weaknesses.
- Missing methodological details.
- Inadequate citations.
- Unclear novelty claims.
- Overstated conclusions.
- Missing reproducibility information.
- Contradictions between sections.

Produce an internal editorial assessment and use it to guide the revisions.

Do not stop after identifying problems. Correct every issue that can be responsibly corrected.

PHASE 2 — SCIENTIFIC ORIGINALITY AND LITERATURE VERIFICATION

Reassess the novelty of the proposed methodology against the scientific literature.

Investigate the closest existing methodologies and publications.

Verify:

- Whether the central contribution is genuinely distinctive.
- Whether similar frameworks already exist.
- Whether important competing approaches have been omitted.
- Whether the literature review adequately represents the field.
- Whether the novelty claims are proportionate to the available evidence.

Strengthen the distinction between established methods and original contributions.

Do not claim that the methodology is the first of its kind unless this can be substantiated.

If the literature reveals a significant precedent, incorporate it and revise the novelty statement accordingly.

PHASE 3 — MATHEMATICAL, STATISTICAL, AND TECHNICAL AUDIT

Examine every mathematical expression, equation, algorithm, statistical procedure, and computational claim.

Verify:

- Mathematical consistency.
- Correct definitions of variables.
- Validity of assumptions.
- Dimensional consistency where applicable.
- Statistical appropriateness.
- Computational feasibility.
- Reproducibility.
- Interpretability.
- Consistency between theoretical claims and actual implementation.

Check whether the reported experiments genuinely support the conclusions.

Do not invent missing mathematical proofs, experimental results, or validation evidence.

If a component is not adequately justified, correct it or explicitly identify what remains unresolved.

PHASE 4 — REBUILD THE SCIENTIFIC NARRATIVE

Optimize the manuscript's intellectual architecture.

The narrative should communicate:

1. An important unresolved scientific problem.
2. Why existing approaches are insufficient for the particular problem being addressed.
3. The precise intellectual breakthrough.
4. The fundamental methodological innovation.
5. How the methodology operates.
6. What evidence supports its effectiveness.
7. What makes the contribution valuable.
8. What remains uncertain.

Make the scientific argument compelling without exaggeration.

Every section should advance the central argument.

Remove repetition, unnecessary complexity, and decorative academic language.

Preserve the originality and intellectual identity of the research.

PHASE 5 — OPTIMIZE EVERY SECTION

Rewrite and finalize every manuscript section.

TITLE

Create a precise, memorable, scientifically credible title that communicates the methodological contribution without making unsupported claims.

ABSTRACT

Produce a concise, high-impact abstract containing the problem, objective, methodological innovation, validation approach, actual results, and implications.

INTRODUCTION

Establish the research problem, relevant literature, methodological gap, and original contribution.

LITERATURE REVIEW

Provide a balanced and current assessment of relevant research, including competing methodologies.

THEORETICAL FOUNDATIONS

Present the conceptual and mathematical foundations clearly and rigorously.

METHODOLOGY

Make the complete procedure precise enough for independent implementation.

RESULTS

Ensure that all reported findings are traceable to actual analyses and evidence.

DISCUSSION

Interpret the findings, distinguish demonstrated advantages from potential advantages, and explain the contribution to the field.

LIMITATIONS

Be transparent about weaknesses and unresolved questions.

CONCLUSION

Present the contribution accurately and explain its scientific significance.

PHASE 6 — REFERENCES AND CITATION FORENSICS

Audit every citation and reference.

Verify, where possible:

- Author names.
- Publication titles.
- Publication years.
- Journal names.
- Volume and issue numbers.
- Page ranges.
- DOI identifiers.
- Publication status.
- Whether the cited source actually supports the associated claim.

Identify nonexistent, incorrect, irrelevant, outdated, or misleading references.

Replace weak references with stronger primary research or authoritative methodological sources where appropriate.

Ensure that every in-text citation has a corresponding reference-list entry and vice versa.

Use the target journal's required referencing style.

Never fabricate references, DOIs, or publication details.

Flag any source that cannot be verified.

PHASE 7 — TABLES, FIGURES, AND SCIENTIFIC PRESENTATION

Audit all tables, diagrams, equations, charts, and figures.

Ensure that:

- Every figure communicates a meaningful scientific concept.
- Every table has a clear purpose.
- All labels are consistent.
- Figures are referenced in the manuscript.
- Captions are informative and self-contained.
- Mathematical notation is consistent.
- Visual representations accurately reflect the methodology.
- Figure resolution and file formats meet journal requirements.

Create or refine publication-quality figures where the available tools permit.

Prepare a complete figure and table inventory.

PHASE 8 — REPRODUCIBILITY AND RESEARCH INTEGRITY

Prepare the manuscript for independent scientific scrutiny.

Verify that it contains adequate information about:

- Data sources.
- Data availability.
- Inclusion and exclusion criteria.
- Analytical procedures.
- Software and package versions.
- Computational requirements.
- Code availability.
- Statistical procedures.
- Research ethics, where applicable.
- Preregistration, where applicable.
- Funding.
- Conflicts of interest.
- Author contributions.

Prepare the relevant statements.

Never invent funding, institutional approval, author identities, affiliations, ethical approvals, registrations, or data availability.

Use clearly marked placeholders where information must be supplied by the authors.

Distinguish between materials that already exist and materials that still need to be created.

PHASE 9 — SELECT THE OPTIMAL TARGET JOURNAL

Identify suitable journals based on:

- Methodological novelty.
- Scientific discipline.
- Scope.
- Research contribution.
- Article type.
- Editorial standards.
- Publication requirements.
- Accessibility and visibility.
- Open-access policies.
- Publication fees.
- Submission eligibility.

Investigate actual journal guidelines and current requirements.

Develop a shortlist of three appropriate journals.

Compare their suitability and select one primary target.

Do not select a journal simply because of its prestige.

Prioritize genuine editorial fit and realistic publication potential.

Do not guarantee acceptance or imply that a journal has endorsed the manuscript.

PHASE 10 — SIMULATE THE FINAL PEER REVIEW

Conduct a final independent review from five perspectives:

1. Scientific editor.
2. Research methodologist.
3. Statistician.
4. Computational and mathematical reviewer.
5. Specialist in the manuscript's scientific domain.

Each reviewer must identify potential grounds for rejection.

Pay particular attention to methodological novelty, validity, reproducibility, experimental evidence, and clarity.

Resolve every correctable criticism.

If a fundamental weakness cannot be resolved without additional experiments or evidence, identify it clearly rather than concealing it.

PHASE 11 — PREPARE THE COMPLETE SUBMISSION PACKAGE

Produce the following deliverables:

A. Final manuscript

A complete, professionally edited, publication-ready manuscript.

B. Title page

Include the title, author information, affiliations, corresponding author details, and other required information. Use placeholders for unknown details.

C. Structured abstract

Adapted to the selected journal's requirements.

D. Keywords

Select relevant, discoverable scientific keywords.

E. Cover letter

Write a persuasive and professionally appropriate letter to the journal editor explaining:

- The research problem.
- The methodological contribution.
- The scientific significance.
- Why the manuscript fits the journal.
- The manuscript's originality.
- Its relevance to the journal's readership.

Do not make unsupported claims or promise acceptance.

F. Highlights

Prepare concise statements of the main contributions if required by the journal.

G. Graphical abstract

Prepare a scientifically accurate visual summary of the research, if appropriate and permitted by the journal.

H. Figures and tables

Provide the final versions, captions, numbering, and specifications.

I. Supplementary materials

Prepare mathematical derivations, extended methodological protocols, pseudocode, additional results, validation procedures, and supplementary figures where available.

J. Declarations

Prepare funding, conflicts of interest, author contributions, ethics, data availability, and code availability statements as applicable.

K. Reporting checklist

Identify applicable reporting guidelines and prepare the required checklist. Do not claim compliance with requirements that have not been satisfied.

L. Submission metadata

Prepare the manuscript title, abstract, keywords, article type, author information, and other required submission fields.

M. Submission checklist

Create a final checklist covering all files, declarations, journal formatting rules, and administrative requirements.

PHASE 12 — FINAL EDITORIAL DECISION

Before declaring the work complete, conduct one final assessment.

Ask:

- Is the central scientific contribution clear?
- Is the methodology sufficiently developed?
- Are its advantages supported by actual evidence?
- Are its limitations honestly represented?
- Are its mathematical foundations defensible?
- Are all citations trustworthy?
- Can independent researchers reproduce the work?
- Is the target journal appropriate?
- Is every required submission document prepared?
- Are there unresolved issues that could cause immediate rejection?

Make the necessary final corrections.

Assign one of these editorial statuses:

1. READY FOR SUBMISSION.
2. READY AFTER AUTHOR INFORMATION IS COMPLETED.
3. READY AFTER ADDITIONAL DOCUMENTATION.
4. NOT READY — SCIENTIFIC OR METHODOLOGICAL ISSUES REMAIN.

Never label a manuscript ready if essential scientific or administrative requirements remain unmet.
